No — **you cannot play Big Walk solo**. It is a **co-op-only game** with no single-player mode. You need at least one other person online to start a session, and the game scales up to 12 players. House House built Big Walk from the ground up around cooperative communication puzzles, so playing alone is not a supported mode — it does not exist in the game. If you are looking for a solo adventure, this is not it.

## Big Walk Is Co-op Only By Design

![Four characters carrying binoculars and a walkie-talkie on a coastal cliff overlooking the ocean](/images/topics/cliff-items.jpg)

Big Walk's entire design depends on having at least two people. The core mechanic is **communication under constraint**: proximity voice chat that fades with distance, muffles through walls, echoes in tunnels, and crackles over walkie-talkies. The puzzles are built around two or more players trying to share information across obstacles — soundproof rooms, distance, darkness, limited tools. A solo player would have no one to communicate with, which would dissolve the entire game.

House House is explicit about this. The official description frames Big Walk as "a hand-crafted cooperative adventure meant to be played start-to-finish with a group of friends." Kotaku's reviewer stressed the same point: this is not a single-player game with optional co-op bolted on — you must play it with at least one other person, and up to eleven others.

There is no middle ground or workaround. No bot fill, no offline solo mode, no way to experience the campaign alone. Every structure that contains a puzzle contains one that, in the reviewer's words, requires more than one person to solve.

## Why There Is No Single-Player Mode

![Characters gathered around a glowing campsite on a dark hillside at night](/images/topics/night-camp.jpg)

The reason there is no solo mode is the reason Big Walk exists at all: its puzzles are **communication puzzles**. Many challenges separate players with soundproof walls or large distances, forcing them to invent signaling systems using flashlights, laser pointers, arm gestures, and walkie-talkies. One memorable puzzle has a player wear a blindfold (a giant golden head) and navigate an obstacle course while teammates shout instructions. None of this works with one person.

Reviewers consistently highlight that Big Walk's magic is the shared, in-the-room feeling of struggling to describe what you see to someone who cannot see it. Strip out the second player and you have stripped out the game. Even the ending leans on this: the finale tests the trust and body language your group built over the whole campaign, which only means something if there were other people there to build it with.

Even the smaller social mechanics assume a group. You cannot paint your own character's colors — a friend has to do it with the paintbrush. From the first tutorial hour to the final puzzle, the game keeps handing you things you physically cannot do alone.

## Can You Play With Strangers Instead?

If you cannot recruit friends, playing with strangers is technically possible — reviewers expect a community to form around connecting Big Walk players, and plenty of people will have a good time that way. But every review points the same direction: this game belongs with people you know.

The campaign builds on itself. The shared language your group invents — your names for the red objects, your signal codes, your inside jokes about who dropped the flashlight off a cliff — is the actual content of Big Walk. A rotating cast of strangers can solve the puzzles, but they will not carry your group's vocabulary with them. So the honest answer for a solo player is: even the "play with strangers" path is really a "make some friends quickly" path.

## Is Big Walk Replayable After The First Run?

Even apart from the co-op requirement, Big Walk is widely described as a **single-use experience** — its power comes from surprise and discovery, and once your group knows the puzzles, a lot of the magic is spent. One reviewer put it bluntly: they did not think they could ever play it a second time, because "once you know the tricks, you can't be surprised by them again." So Big Walk is neither solo-friendly nor particularly replayable after the first campaign.

There are caveats worth knowing before you treat it as strictly one-and-done. You do not need to complete every puzzle to reach the credits, so a group can keep exploring and mopping up leftover challenges after the story ends. Reviewers have also hinted, without spoiling anything, that a **post-game something** exists beyond the ending that groups make time to return for. And the game tracks a set of **12 achievements** for groups who want a completionist reason to linger.

Pace your single run accordingly. Reviewers spent close to twenty hours wandering the island with their groups, and the recommended rhythm is about one night a week for roughly a month. That is the shape of the experience Big Walk was designed for — one sustained campaign with the same people, not a game you reinstall every season.

None of that turns Big Walk into a replayable live-service game. It means the one big walk has a little more at the edges than the credits suggest. Plan for one great campaign, treat anything after as a bonus, and savor it.

## One Friend Is Enough To Start

The good news for the almost-solo player: the minimum group is two, and House House explicitly designed for it. The 2-player mode is billed as "a focused co-op experience," with puzzles adjusted slightly so a pair can solve everything. A duo can carry the entire campaign start to finish without ever recruiting a third.

So if you have exactly one willing friend — a partner, a sibling, a regular co-op buddy — Big Walk works. What it has no mode for is the number one. The jump from one player to two is the jump from unsupported to fully supported, and it is the only jump you have to make.

## What Solo Players Should Do Instead

If you are primarily a solo player, you have two realistic paths with Big Walk:

- **Assemble a group for one focused campaign.** Big Walk is designed to be played start-to-finish with a consistent group over roughly a month of weekly sessions. Treat it like a book club: recruit a few friends, commit to a regular night, and finish it together.
- **Skip it if solo-only is your hard rule.** There is no way to make Big Walk a solo game, and nothing in the official features or reviews suggests a solo mode is coming.

Crossplay helps with the first path — you can pull friends from PC, Mac, PS5, and Switch 2 into one session, so platform fragmentation is not the blocker. PS5 friends could even claim the game free at launch via PS Plus, which lowers the ask. The blocker is purely whether you can convene the people.

The timing argument for doing it now rather than "eventually": Big Walk's magic is spoiler-fragile. Every friend who plays later is a friend who has already heard the stories, the puzzle tricks, and the ending. Gathering your group while the game is fresh is not just scheduling — it is how you protect the one run you get.

## Summary

Big Walk **cannot be played solo** — it is co-op only, requiring at least one friend online (up to 12). The entire game is built on communication puzzles that need two or more people, so no single-player mode exists. It is also largely a one-time experience rather than a replayable one, with only leftover puzzles, a hinted post-game something, and 12 achievements beyond the credits. Gather a group for a single start-to-finish campaign, or pick a different game if you only play alone.
