## The IGN Big Walk Wiki

The best current wiki destination is **IGN's Big Walk wiki at https://www.ign.com/wikis/big-walk**. It is maintained by IGN's guides staff rather than open community editing, which makes it more reliable than a typical day-one fan wiki. Its pages have been updated actively — several were refreshed in the week after launch — and they cover the questions players hit early on:

- **Beginner's Tips and Tricks** — session sizes, movement techniques, and setup for a first playthrough.
- **Frequently Asked Questions** — answers to the questions the game's minimal tutorials never address.
- **How to Change Color** — the paint room and paintbrush in the tutorial area, and why a friend has to paint you.
- **How to Use the Radio** — the green transmitters that unlock radio stations, all seven of them, spread across the map.
- **How to Get a Backpack** — the circular blue structures that hold backpacks and belts, including the one near the Red Tower.
- **How to Get a Map** — unlocking the Red Tower, depositing five beans, shaping the key, and reaching the big blue tunnel with the three-dimensional map inside.
- **How to Save** — host-only saves, automatic saving, and what happens to held items between sessions.

Beyond IGN's wiki, there is **no dedicated, mature fan wiki for Big Walk yet**. Whether one grows on the usual fan-wiki platforms is to be confirmed; for now, professional coverage is the stronger reference.

The existence of wiki-style demand is easy to understand: Big Walk begins with essentially no instructions. You start in a tutorial playground with your controls pinned up as posters, no objective given, and no map in hand — the map itself is something your group has to unlock. A game that deliberately withholds its own manual naturally sends players searching for reference pages, which is exactly the gap the IGN guides and sites like this one fill.

## Be Careful With Early Fan Wikis

A warning about early fan-contributed wikis: in the first weeks after launch, they are **often incomplete, inconsistent, or speculative**. Anyone can edit them, and during the launch rush, well-meaning editors sometimes add unverified claims, mislabeled puzzles, or invented details. Cross-check anything you read on a fresh wiki against official sources or multiple player reports before treating it as fact.

This is especially true for numbers — player caps, puzzle counts, completion times, trophy requirements — which early wikis frequently get wrong. Big Walk adds a complication of its own: because groups invent their own names for the game's objects (beans, gourds, eggy things), different pages may describe the same item with different words. Prefer information that cites a source or matches the consensus across several players.

One practical check before you trust any page: look at its last-updated date. The IGN wiki's pages carry recent update timestamps from the week after launch, which is a good sign the information reflects the shipping game. A wiki page that has not been touched since launch week, on a game this new, deserves extra skepticism.

## Why Big Walk Resists A Traditional Wiki

Part of Big Walk's design limits how useful a traditional wiki can be:

- **The puzzles are communication-based**, not knowledge-based. Reading a walkthrough does not really "solve" a puzzle the way it would in a logic or inventory game, because the challenge is coordinating with your group in the moment.
- **The experience is emergent** — reviewers stress that the best moments are unscripted and unique to each group. A wiki can catalog the world but cannot capture what playing it is like.
- **It is largely a single-use experience** — once your group knows the puzzles, the magic is spent, so spoiling yourself via a wiki before playing carries a real cost.

Use a wiki for reference (what tools exist, how progression works, platform details), not as a substitute for playing.
