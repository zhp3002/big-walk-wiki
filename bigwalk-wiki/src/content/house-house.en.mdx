**House House is the Australian independent game studio behind Big Walk**, and the developer of the breakout hit *Untitled Goose Game*. They are a small team known for short, distinctive, character-driven games that turn a simple mechanic into a memorable experience. With Big Walk — publishing with Panic — they released their cooperative communication adventure on August 4, 2026, applying the same design philosophy of restraint and charm to a multiplayer puzzle game. Here is the background on House House, their track record, and how Big Walk fits their style.

## Who Is House House?

House House is an **independent game studio based in Melbourne, Australia**. They are known as a small, creatively driven team that favors focused, original concepts over scale or trend. Their games share a recognizable sensibility: clean art direction, a sense of humor, approachable mechanics, and a refusal to pad a game beyond its natural length. This identity has made them one of the more respected indie studios working today.

For Big Walk, House House partnered with **publisher Panic** (known for both games and the Playdate handheld), which handles publishing duties while House House retains creative control. The pairing shows up throughout the game's press coverage, where screenshots and videos are credited jointly to House House and Panic. This developer-publisher relationship is consistent with how House House has brought their work to broad audiences before.

## The Studio's Track Record

House House is best known for:

- ***Untitled Goose Game*** (2019) — a comedic stealth-puzzle game where you play a goose annoying villagers. It became a viral hit, won a BAFTA, and established House House's reputation for turning one strong mechanic into a beloved, widely discussed game.
- ***Push Me Pull You*** — an earlier local-multiplayer game that already showed the studio's interest in cooperative, physical, communication-heavy play — a clear ancestor of Big Walk's design concerns.

This history matters for understanding Big Walk: the studio did not randomly land on a co-op communication game. They have been exploring local, shared-space, goofy multiplayer for years, and Big Walk is the online evolution of those ideas. Reviewers who played both games note the continuity directly — the goose game's full-circle ending, in which the goose steals a tiny bell and runs it back through every area it visited, set expectations for Big Walk's finale that the new game then exceeded.

## How Big Walk Fits House House's Style

Big Walk is recognizably a House House game in several ways:

- **One strong mechanic, fully explored** — where *Untitled Goose Game* was "be a nuisance," Big Walk is "coordinate through constrained communication." Both build an entire game around a single idea rather than stacking systems.
- **Charm and humor** — the colorful, slightly surreal world and the emergent comedy of group chaos are very much in the studio's voice. Even the celebration is charming: every solved puzzle ends with a party-horn honk and a burst of confetti.
- **Accessibility and brevity** — House House favors games that do not overstay their welcome. Big Walk's roughly 20-hour, start-to-finish campaign fits their preference for finite, crafted experiences over endless live-service loops.
- **Restraint** — no quest markers, no opening map, no hand-holding. The studio trusts players to figure things out, just as the goose game trusted players to experiment.

The official feature list is perhaps the most House House thing about the game: "Talking!", "Walking!", "Arms!!", and "An ending" — a hand-crafted cooperative adventure meant to be played start-to-finish with a group of friends. That is a studio confidently describing exactly the game it intended to make.

## From Single-Player To Co-op

The notable shift with Big Walk is that House House has moved from a primarily **single-player experience to a cooperative online one**. Where *Untitled Goose Game* was you alone against the villagers, Big Walk requires a group of 2 to 12 friends communicating through proximity voice chat, with voices that fade across distances, muffle through walls, echo down corridors, and crackle through walkie-talkies. This is a meaningful evolution for the studio, but it stays true to their core interest in how players interact with systems and each other.

House House arrived at this point at a moment when the so-called "friendslop" wave — indie games built to be played with friends — was already in full swing. Reviewers noted that Big Walk sits apart from that label: where much of the wave is loose and hangout-shaped, Big Walk is clean, aggressively earnest, and carefully constructed. The studio brought a craftsman's discipline to a genre that usually improvises, which is precisely the reputation they built with the goose game.

Reviewers note that House House's knack for emergent comedy translates beautifully to multiplayer — the goose game's slapstick becomes Big Walk's communicative chaos, with the same emphasis on player-driven moments over scripted gags. One review captured the design principle underneath: a good puzzle game makes a player feel like a genius, and Big Walk is built to make a whole group feel like a collective genius. That is a House House idea as much as a puzzle-design idea.

The ending extends the same thinking. Without spoiling it: where the goose game closed with a joyful full-circle chase, Big Walk's finale tests everything your group has learned about communicating under constraints, and reviewers describe it as genuinely moving — with a post-game surprise waiting beyond the credits for groups who are not ready to stop.

## Why The Developer Matters For Your Purchase

Knowing the developer helps you set expectations. If you liked *Untitled Goose Game*'s tone and focus, Big Walk offers that sensibility scaled up to a cooperative campaign — with the same understanding that the game is a crafted, finite experience to be savored rather than grinded. If you found House House's previous work too slight or short, know that Big Walk is substantially longer and more ambitious, but still prioritizes a curated experience over content volume.

It also tells you about post-launch support: House House is not a studio that churns out live-service content updates, and Big Walk shipped as a complete game. There is even a light post-game activity for groups who finish, but nobody should buy expecting an evolving content platform. What launched on August 4, 2026 is the game.

Finally, remember that Big Walk carried unusual expectations for a small studio's follow-up: after a viral hit, everyone watching House House's next move measured it against the goose. That the studio's answer was a 2-to-12 player communication game — and that reviewers received it as one of the best games of 2026 — says a lot about how much faith they earned.

Knowing the developer tells you what to expect: a complete, curated co-op game in the studio's distinctive voice.
