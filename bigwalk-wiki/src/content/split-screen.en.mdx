## What Big Walk Is Built For: Online Co-op

Big Walk is, first and foremost, an **online co-op game**. Its defining mechanic — proximity-based voice chat that fades, muffles, and echoes based on the virtual world — assumes each player has their own screen and their own audio perspective. The puzzles hinge on players being separated by walls, distance, or darkness, which is hard to replicate meaningfully when everyone is looking at the same screen.

Crossplay extends this online focus: friends on PC, Mac, PS5, and Switch 2 join the same session over the internet. The official features list emphasizes "Play online with friends on other platforms," not local multiplayer. So the safe assumption is that Big Walk is an online co-op game, and **local couch co-op is either absent or unconfirmed**.

The rest of the feature list tells the same story. It covers talking, walking, arm gestures, tools and toys, the flexible 2-12 player count, crossplay, and "an ending" — nowhere does it mention split-screen, local play, or a shared-screen mode. For a hand-crafted game whose marketing is this thorough about how you communicate, the silence on local co-op is meaningful.

## Why Everyone Asks About Split-Screen

The question is understandable. Big Walk looks like a couch game: goofy characters built from stacked colored balls, bright toy-like monuments, an obvious party energy, and a place among the recent wave of "play with your friends" indie hits. Everything about its vibe suggests a living room and a shared screen.

But the vibe and the architecture point in opposite directions. The game's communication model — per-player proximity audio, separated teammates, information one player can see and another cannot — is an online design from the ground up. That tension is exactly why the split-screen question keeps coming up, and why the honest answer stays "unconfirmed" instead of a comfortable yes or no.

## Is There A Split-Screen Mode?

A dedicated **split-screen mode has not been announced or shown in the marketing**, and it does not appear in the official features list. Review materials describe sessions played online across separate devices, with players talking through in-game proximity audio. Treat the presence of split-screen as **to be confirmed** until House House or the store listings explicitly say otherwise — but the design of the game (separated players, individual audio perspectives) makes split-screen an awkward fit, so do not count on it.

It is worth being precise about what "unconfirmed" means here. It does not mean split-screen is hidden in the game, and it does not mean House House has ruled it out. It means no official source in front of us says one way or the other, and a wiki should not fill that silence with a guess — in either direction.

If split-screen is a dealbreaker for you, wait for an explicit confirmation from the official store page before buying, or assume it is online-only.

## Local Co-op And The Voice Chat Problem

Even setting aside split-screen, **local co-op faces a structural problem** in Big Walk: the proximity voice chat. The game's audio is positional and player-specific — your voice muffles to *me* when you are behind a wall, but you hear yourself normally. If two people share a room and a screen, that per-player audio perspective breaks down, because the sound is coming out of one set of speakers into one physical space.

This is likely a core reason House House focused on online play. The puzzles and the voice system both assume individual audio environments per player, which is naturally provided by everyone wearing their own headset in their own location. On top of the audio, several puzzle types simply stop working when both players can see the same view: a puzzle about describing what you can see to someone who cannot see it has no tension on a shared screen.

The reviews are full of moments that prove the point. One reviewer describes a wall that lets two players see each other but not hear each other, and the small triumph of working out how to pass information through it. Another describes the panic of losing your group's only flashlight and walkie-talkie off a cliff at night. Those stories only exist because each player's senses were their own — which is precisely what a shared screen and a shared speaker cannot reproduce.

## How To Play Big Walk With Someone In The Same Room

![Two characters sitting on a bench at a lit train platform at dusk](/images/topics/train-platform.jpg)

If you want to play Big Walk with a friend or partner who is physically with you, the realistic setup is **two devices side by side** — two PCs, a PC and a laptop, a PS5 and a Switch 2, and so on — each running its own copy of the game, connected via crossplay, with each player using their own headphones. That preserves the per-player proximity audio the game depends on.

This is more demanding than classic couch co-op, but it is the way to get the intended experience locally. It also means each of you needs their own copy — one purchase does not cover a shared-screen pair. Sharing a single screen would undermine the communication puzzles that are the whole point of the game.

The upside is that this setup works today, with no waiting on confirmation. Crossplay between the four platforms is a confirmed, shipped feature, so two devices side by side is simply a crossplay session where the players happen to be within arm's reach. Sessions are organized around hosting and Join Codes, so whoever owns the more reliable machine can host while the other joins — the physical room you share makes no difference to the network.

For couples and housemates, this arrangement keeps the comfort of playing on the same couch while preserving the game's separation: you still lose each other in the dark and muffle through walls — just with someone you can elbow when the session ends.
