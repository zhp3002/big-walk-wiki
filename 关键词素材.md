To know something's name is to understand it — at least just a little, be it the thing itself, or the world around it. In Big Walk, there are lots of things yet unnamed. You will certainly recognize trees, mountains, that dang cowbell. But there are plenty of things you won’t: the red, peanut-shaped objects my group calls a bean, which makes the structures they must be delivered to the bean towers. To us, the underground tunnel system is the backrooms, and the glowing medicine balls that light paths throughout the world are simply orbs – but not to be confused with the Big Orb, a mysterious and ominous structure looming in the distance. Big Walk is a co-op-only puzzle adventure that relies heavily on shared experience, shared language. Its genius lies in how it pushes you to decipher the logic behind the clever puzzles spread around its open world in a way that only you and your companions will understand. With all the twists and limitations it imposes, it’s unlike any other multiplayer game out there.

Big Walk is a puzzle game, and the puzzle is communication. Everyone is initially dropped into a playground of sorts with some colorful, blocklike structures to climb on, light switches to flip, and your controls pinned up as posters. I call this place home. Crucially, you are given no objective. We fiddle about with the buttons, obnoxiously clang the cowbell. We test out our movement options – mostly arm gestures – by looking at our shadows. Eventually, we head out into a lifelike world with lush foliage and waves breaking on the beach; it's a direct contrast to our charming, goofy characters, who are, essentially, a stack of three colored balls with googly eyes, stick arms, and twig legs (and a nose that moves like a mouth as we speak in-game).

When someone asked what we're supposed to do, what the objective of this game was, I couldn’t really answer that. And so we walked. But here's what I knew at this point: Big Walk has walking and puzzles. That, and outside of arm flailing or typing out written messages, we can only communicate through proximity-based voice chat. This means your voice is muffled when you're behind a wall, and echoes when you're in a vast tunnel. If someone wanders too far off, I can no longer hear them, and as a game that can only be played with others, it’s “cooperative” in the very sense of the term. There's no way to play Big Walk alone, and it supports up to 12 players with different settings for different sized groups: two, three, or four-plus, which adjusts puzzles slightly to suit the number of people in a session. Big Walk cleverly takes advantage of the essential limitation of proximity chat, and it wouldn't be the same without it.

Venturing out, my group of three walks together along desert terrain and up a cliffside until we find a button on a platform. We originally think nothing happens when it's pushed, but someone notices a faint red light flashing in the distance. It illuminates each time it's pressed, so we send someone in its direction to see what’s out there. There's a red bean in a box, which opens when the button is pushed. He grabs it and rushes back. No one knows what to do with it but it seems important, so we hold onto it (or try our best until someone drops it while falling off a cliff). Through that simple opening task, we start to get it: This puzzle, this bean, they’re teaching us something about the world and what we need to do within it. We're starting to create our own language.

Each piece of this map is cleverly designed to nudge your group to build something together, forms of communication that feel like only you understand. We keep walking, keep exploring and finding puzzles that require real teamwork, and continue to build our language. My group played in several sessions across a few days: a core group of three, with some stragglers jumping in here and there. We spent nearly 20 delightful hours romping around. It's easy for people to jump in and out of the session, popping in to adventure for a bit. There is a kink in this system, though. The save file is tied to the player who originally hosted the game. For my group, that was me. If my companions wanted to play when I wasn't there, they'd have to start a new save file—and a new big walk. This may very well be by design; developer House House wants a group to stick together. But I felt bad when someone wanted to play, but I couldn't.

Big Walk's world runs on a day-night cycle, with sun-soaked days and pitch-dark nights. And in the latter, a different kind of communication is built. Early on, my friend Riley dropped our only flashlight off a cliff, immediately after I accidentally kicked our walkie-talkie down the same one. We spent a not-insignificant amount of time looking for our tools because, without them, we could easily lose each other in the dead of night. (Even more so when looking for Riley, who got stuck in a hole while searching for our gear). Perhaps in another game, losing your tools or falling in a ditch would be frustrating. Not so here. While movement can be finicky at times, and you will briefly get stuck in spots, Big Walk often felt even more impactful when things were going very, very wrong.

We spent plenty of time doing Big Walk's actual puzzles, which are scattered around the map and usually embedded in big, brightly colored structures: a green horror movie house, an orange colosseum, a blue sound stage. Some of them are relatively easy, like throwing an orb into a basketball hoop multiple times, or playing a game of telephone to identify a specific sequence of shapes or buttons to push. But there are mechanical challenges as well, ones that require platforming and precise movement or throws. There's an entertaining puzzle that requires one person to put on a blindfold (which is actually a big, weird gold head that blocks their view) and navigate through an obstacle course. The others then have to yell instructions at them about where to go and what to do; the scariest part of this was figuring out how to time a jump over a gap. These challenges don't necessarily get progressively harder, because you're free to do whichever puzzle in whatever order you discover them. Some are certainly difficult, but never cruelly so. Big Walk always gives you the tools you need to solve them, so there's no danger of a dramatic learning curve.

One particularly inventive puzzle had my friend Chris locked in a soundproof room trying to relay little musical jingles to us so we could match that to stereos playing the corresponding melodies. Another forced us to split up onto three platforms that were pretty far away from each other and correctly time a synchronized button push. There are a couple puzzles with similar ideas, and the problem was often that we only had two walkie-talkies amongst us. So, we used our laser pointer to signify a countdown or our arms to send a silent message. Little by little, we created a sort of body language cipher to help solve matching puzzles and timing-based challenges alike, and it was a thrill to naturally develop our own system of visual cues.

Each of these puzzles rewards us with another red bean we have to take care of as we bring it back to a bean tower for deposit. Once you've found enough beans for a given tower, you get a special key to unlock something new and helpful: stuff you otherwise couldn't figure out how to operate, like a stalled train or an island-spanning chair lift. And in this progression, Big Walk further embeds the ethos that these discoveries are yours to make.

Big Walk's puzzles are always sharp, and always fun. The use of proximity chat makes the process of solving them feel special in a way I haven’t experienced in a game for a long time. Despite the recent wave of co-op games with an emphasis on hanging out with your friends, it feels so new to play in this particular way, as if we were all physically in this virtual space together. Video games often make us feel like superheroes, like the world revolves around our individual characters. In Big Walk, relationships and language are that superpower. The world doesn't revolve around me, but us.


So much of Big Walk's fun is found in the spaces between the puzzles, too. One of my favorite moments was this time Riley and I wandered away from Chris to go gallivanting in nature without care. This would not have been a big deal… if Riley and I did not have both walkie-talkies. Notoriously bad at navigating, we got wildly lost, even more so after we tumbled down the mountains once again. We were lost for nearly an hour, maybe longer. We went to the train to honk its horn. We tried to follow the coastline, then the train line itself, to make our way back to the place where we first realized we'd lost Chris. Then, a flare shot up in the distance. We walked toward the sound and eventually the direction of the green hue the blast gave off. That's where we found him, at a bean puzzle, firing off flares. Eventually, we sent Chris off to follow the curve of the flare and the bean was waiting at the end of it, like a pot of gold at the end of a rainbow. Riley and I certainly couldn't have been trusted to go find it.

I spent a lot of time playing Big Walk over the last week, but I also spent a lot of time thinking about it, and talking about it with the folks I played with. I'm thinking a lot about names, and the power they hold. In the book Braiding Sweetgrass: Indigenous Wisdom, Scientific Knowledge, and the Teachings of Plants, author and scientist Robin Wall Kimmerer writes that names are how we, as humans, build relationships with each other and "the living world." She muses on what it might be like to not know the names of things around you. Kimmerer writes, "I think it would be a little scary and disorienting – like being lost in a foreign city where you can’t read the street signs." Big Walk's opening feels a little bit like that. Its island is not yet a place with which we've built a relationship, or developed a language. The moment you understand this, Big Walk changes. And what a special thing it is to name this world with friends.

Verdict
Big Walk is full of beautiful emergent moments as you find solutions to cleverly built puzzles and adventure between them in its vast open world. Falling down a mountain at night and scrambling to find your dropped gear, finding your friends from a flare in the distance, or developing the language to both name things in its world and build a process to figure out its most complex puzzles is all part of the magic that makes Big Walk unlike any other co-op game. The myriad ways it pushes you and your friends, placing limitations that test your problem solving in ways as challenging as they are charming – that’s what defines this experience. But that intrinsic reward of freeing the next red bean to put it in its place atop the next big bean tower is just as special.
Big Walk is a cooperative multiplayer adventure about teamwork and talking.

Set out with your friends through a wide-open world full of challenges, puzzles, and discoveries. You’ll need to work together to find your way around, stay in contact using an assortment of tools and toys, and figure out new ways to communicate when you suddenly find yourself speechless.

Adventuring aside, there’s plenty of time to just hang out. Sit and watch the sunset, or steal your friend’s binoculars and kick them into the ocean. Spending time with friends is the best part of a big walk.

Love or hate the terminology, we’ve recently seen a rise in something folks are calling “friendslop”—generally indie games intended to be played with friends that encourage communication, teamwork, and silliness. Games like Meccha Chameleon, Among Us, and Peak are prime examples, and have gained rapid popularity thanks both to being very well-designed, fun games, and also to their facilitation of positive human relationships. At a time when everyone is feeling increasingly isolated, these games encourage us to play together.

I guess, by this definition, Big Walk is another prime example of friendslop. But I just can’t bring myself to use that term for it, especially with the somewhat messy connotations of the “slop” suffix. There’s something about Big Walk that defies the “slop”ness of the word: something clean and clear, something aggressively earnest and even loving. Whatever that something is, it’s been making me tear up every time I have thought back on moments in my own Big Walk. It’s made me want to message the friends I walked with, excitedly beginning with “Hey, remember when-?”

In Big Walk, you and your friends go on a big walk. It’s set on an uninhabited island: you and your companions are the only ones there. There are lots of trees, a few mountains of varying heights, valleys, rivers, coasts—you know, things you see on a hike. There are no signs or trail markers, no wildlife to be seen, though you can hear birds.

Big Walk 35
© House House
There are large, Fischer-Price-looking monuments spaced out across the landscape, brightly colored, built like giant toys. Normal stuff.


Inside almost all of them is a puzzle, always one that requires more than one person to solve and almost always one that requires thoughtful communication and teamwork. For instance, the very first puzzle we encountered in the tutorial area was a giant crane with a button on top, far out of our reach. To get to it, the six of us had to stack on top of one another, with the bottom person lining the tower up correctly and the top person hitting the button. Another very early one had one person push a button to light up and activate an objective across the valley, and the other person had to follow the light and run over to the correct spot. These were simple, early game puzzles, but everything grew increasingly complex from there.

The reward for solving most puzzles is a weird little nub that our group unfortunately immediately dubbed a “nipple.” You can call it what you like. The goal, very roughly, of Big Walk is to collect a lot of nipples (sorry, I won’t do it again).


In this regard, I guess I should describe Big Walk as a puzzle game primarily. It is certainly the puzzle game that finally made it click for me what it is that makes a really good puzzle game. It’s not about puzzles being difficult, or easy enough, or especially intricate, or anything like that. A good puzzle game makes you, the player, feel like a genius. Big Walk does one even better. It puzzles are almost universally so well-paced and well-designed that it makes a group of people feel like a collective genius. It even celebrates that genius appropriately: I love that every time you finish a puzzle, there’s a little party horn “honk!” noise and a bunch of confetti is spit out at you. A mini-celebration, every time, with you and your friends jumping up and down and waving your arms and yelling into the mics because you got it, you did it!

Big Walk 26

Big Walk is also explicitly a cooperative game. By that I do not mean that it is a single-player game with optional co-op. I mean you must, must play it with at least one other friend, up to 11 others if you like, and ideally at least five or six if you want the richest experience possible. You don’t need to play it with the same people every time: the start of each session lets the host choose between a game for 2 people, 3 people, and 4+, which adjusts some of the puzzles to ensure they’re solvable by the number of folks available. I don’t recommend having different people hop in and out of Big Walk (though you can), because it is very much an experience that builds on itself, and the knowledge you gain in one session will almost certainly be helpful in the next. But the ability to adjust is great if one of your friends can’t make a play session on a given night.

And I do specify, you should play this with your friends. You can play it with strangers—I have no doubt there will be a wonderful little community established around connecting folks to play Big Walk together, and a lot of people will have a good time that way. But if you can, play it with people you know and love dearly. Get them to make time.


This is necessary not just mechanically, but also thematically, because Big Walk is fundamentally a game about communication, and finding ways to do it within different limitations. The little dudes you control can gesture: up, forward, and to the side. They can sit and crouch and jump. Different items you pick up throughout the hike can facilitate communication: there are lights you can turn on and off, cowbells to ring, flare guns, walkie-talkies, and so forth. You can only ever hold one thing in your hands at a time, though, and some items are rarer than others, so nothing is as simple as just handing out walkie-talkies and promising to stay in touch if you get lost.

Because you will get lost, even if you think you won’t, even if you swear up and down you’ll stay together. Someone will see something neat and stray off into the trees. Your leader will run too far ahead. It gets dark at night and you’ll lose track of people. A few of your dumbass friends will chase after the flare gun you kicked into the trees minutes before because you were sick of them firing it off every time it reloaded, and then you’ll have to go find them again (following the flare from the gun, of course). You know how it is.

In this regard, Big Walk is as much a game about loneliness as it is about connection. While its highest highs have you trying not to inadvertently spit out your drink all over your keyboard from laughter, there’s also a profundity in quiet moments in the dark, alone, unsure if your friends have a walkie-talkie with which to hear you calling, unable to find a light or a flare gun, trying to find your way back to where the fun is, where your friends are. Please play this game using the in-game proximity voice chat rather than a Discord call. The latter takes away a shocking amount of Big Walk’s impact.

Big Walk is at its best when you and your friends are very stupid. I can honestly say I haven’t laughed as hard as I laughed playing Big Walk in months. It wasn’t about anything video game-y happening at that moment, specifically. It was about what Big Walk facilitated: these wonderful, memorable, goofy moments with people I love. Fellow Kotaku writer Zack Zwiezen singing “Life Is a Highway” off-key into the walkie-talkie. Everyone picking each other up and chucking each other off the tops of tall towers. The thing is, I don’t want to sit here and tell you about every single magical moment I had in Big Walk. I don’t want to tell you about any of them! Because I so badly want you to go play this and experience every single one without knowing what’s coming. So much of the fun of Big Walk is in the surprise: when you’re wandering lost through the woods at night, following the dim light of your friend’s flashlight, and suddenly come upon a towering blue spiral, or a giant jungle gym, or a tidy little house, or some other brightly colored monument rising out of the landscape. I want you to have every last moment of discovery, where your friends and you are on opposite sides of a wall that lets you see one another, but not hear, and suddenly it clicks with one of you how you can communicate necessary information. I want you to be surprised both by the thrill of solving puzzles, and also by the quiet joy of finding a place that exists for no particular reason at all other than to draw your group’s eye and encourage them to take a beat and rest, together.

I have exactly one complaint about Big Walk: sometimes, the walking part is bad. Specifically, when you’re trying to go uphill or when you hit an area of rocky, uneven ground. You could say, “Well, Reb, this is simply part of going on a hike,” but it is not part of going on a hike to get your body wedged between two regular-sized rocks and become unable to move at all, or to be mysteriously unable to get on top of a knee-high boulder, or to be incapable of walking through a person-sized space while holding a whiteboard. These little bits were frustrating when they happened, which was multiple times per walk. They were also quickly forgotten about the second something new caught my interest.

In House House’s last game, Untitled Goose Game, I remember being thrilled by the ending, in which the goose steals a tiny bell from a tower and runs it back through all the areas it’s visited already, chased by angry townspeople, until it reaches a large pile of bells you saw at the beginning and drops it in. It’s such a wonderful, full-circle moment, joyful and satisfying. I was hoping, based on the tidiness of that ending, that Big Walk would have a similarly good conclusion. I was not disappointed. In fact, I was elated. The ending of Big Walk, its final encounter (dare I call it a boss battle?!) and denouement, is fantastic and worth experiencing. I think I spent the final hour of Big Walk happy-crying on and off as my friends and I worked our way through an utterly magnificent final puzzle gauntlet and saw what lay on the other side.

There is also a post-game something that we are making time to do next week. I cannot stop thinking about it and wondering what we’ll discover. I’m really happy there was something beyond the end of Big Walk, because I’m so, so sad to leave it. I don’t think I could ever play this game a second time. It’s not “friendslop.” Its magic is brilliant, and it also is single-use. Once you know the tricks, you can’t be surprised by them again. But oh, the memory of being surprised that first and only time!

Everyone is busy, life is hard, and the world is falling apart. There is no better time to call up your friends, get everyone to give up one night a week for about a month or so, and use that time to go on a Big Walk together. I hope it makes you all as happy as it made us.

Big Walk is one of the best games of 2026. I explain why in my Big Walk review, but if you’re reading this, you don’t seem to need me to convince you to play it; you’re already considering it. Big Walk is an open-world co-op puzzle-adventure with few rules and no quest markers, and part of the fun is in getting lost and exploring the world. I definitely don’t want to rob you of that experience.

However, since beating the game, I’ve heard from others who are getting stumped by some aspects of House House’s latest project. So I’ve put together some Big Walk tips meant to iron out some of the details the game doesn’t clearly tell you.

Focus on one tower at a time…
The game begins with a tutorial section of the island. You’ll need to solve four puzzles, unlock and shape a key, then use it to lower a footbridge that wraps around a mountain. At that point, you can pretty much go anywhere. What you should do is focus on one of the colored mountaintop towers at a time. 


There are red, blue, green, and yellow towers. Filling in each tower’s console with enough puzzle MacGuffins (my group took to calling them “eggy things”) means you’ll unlock whatever major item comes with a tower’s completion. I won’t spoil what each of these towers gives you, but there is arguably a best place to start. For more on that, keep reading.

…And the red tower first
The order in which you complete these towers is totally up to you, but Big Walk gently nudges you to complete the red tower first, by way of starting you so close to it. It’s a good suggestion due to the reward that comes with completing it. 


It’s going to make the rest of the game easier, and though that’s true of any tower reward, I’d argue it’s most true of the red tower. So start there, fill in five eggy things, and unlock a big prize.

Check out these blue structures for helpful items
On your travels, you’ll notice quite a few buildings that look like this blue one in the image. Inside each one will be either a backpack or a belt, on which you can store one item each. This means, along with your hands, each person can carry up to three items.


Advertisement

Unlike your hands, those in your backpack or belt also can’t be dropped when you plummet a great distance, so they’re super reliable. Just know that the game’s intense co-op design means you can’t equip or unequip these items or those they store without a friend’s help.

Be mindful of what you’re holding when you close the game
Big Walk does tell you about this, but it’s easy to miss. In the gym area where each session begins, there’s a Lost and Found table. On it will be whatever items you and your group were holding when the most recent session ended. This means, if you find, say, a flashlight that you want to have from the get-go next time you play, make sure someone is holding it when the host ends the session. It’ll appear there at Lost and Found to make it easy to find again. And if you’re wondering, yes, your backpack, belt, and the items they carry count as held items, too.


If you forget to do this, don’t worry; items will spawn back at where you first found them, so you’ll at least know where to go and grab it again. 

You can communicate across long distances with more than just walkie-talkies
Naturally, a walkie-talkie is a great way to stay in chatting range of your group when you split up. But it’s far from the only way. At the gym, you’ll find signs that you can write messages on, which can be read from a distance with spyglasses or binoculars. You can climb to any tower and use the radio stations to chat with people who have walkie-talkies, or fire off a flare that’s always atop the towers to alert others to your location. 


You can even use things like flashlights and the large bouncy bulbs found all over the island to communicate via signals. You don’t need to know Morse Code in that case–though it would be a neat way to do it. You just need to establish your own terminology with friends, like one blinking light for yes, one solid light for no–things of that nature.

Learn the train’s schedule to plan your journey
Once you get the train moving (which I won’t spoil how you do), you’ll find it never stops moving unless someone is on it holding down the brake. That means you’re best to learn when it’s arriving, which you can do easily at any train station. Each of them features this real-time look at where the train is on the island, so you’ll know whether it makes sense to wait a few minutes for it to take you away, or if you’re best to head off on foot. 


The train, like the ski lift you can also access, isn’t particularly fast, but it’s there to give you a new perspective on the island, help you spot more landmarks, and sometimes get you to a place more directly than hiking.

Think outside the box
With dozens of puzzles to complete, Big Walk will test your mental mettle in many different ways. Some of them can seem obvious–one of the first puzzles my group encountered was essentially basketball. But others will surely stump you for much longer. One of the ingenious things Big Walk does is how it’s sometimes so devilishly simple. 


Make sure you’re considering all angles to a puzzle–speaking figuratively and sometimes literally, communicating clearly with your group, and trying to spot hints at a solution. Everything has a purpose. Nothing is there by accident. There are many puzzles in Big Walk where the answer is staring right at you; you just have to know how to stare back.

You won’t need to complete every puzzle to see the credits but…
Once you really get a lay of the land, you may find yourself wondering, “Will we have to complete all of these puzzles? Yes and no. If you want to beat the game, you’ll need to complete almost all of them. You could end it there, and the ending is one of the best parts, so you’ll surely be pleased to have reached that point.

However, if you’re finding the game as magical as I do, you may be inspired to keep going with the rest of the puzzles. There’s good reason to do that, too, but naturally, I won’t spoil that for you here. For more on the puzzle game, don’t miss how to get shiny and how to solve the 4166, 1899 puzzle.

From a distance, Big Walk doesn’t look like the kind of video game that’s out to deliver a big emotional payoff. It’s a chilled out co-op game that mostly tasks you and your friends with solving puzzles scattered around an open-world island environment. Any story here is the one you invent with your friend(s) while goofing around. And yet, the “ending” of Big Walk left me speechless — in both a figurative and literal sense. The remarkable sequence snaps House House’s excellent game in place, elevating a carefree multiplayer adventure into one of the most emotionally affecting experiences I’ve ever had with a video game.

For the bulk of Big Walk, you follow a clear structure. The island you’re exploring has multiple towers, each of which holds a key locked in place by a mechanism. You can free those keys by collecting red gourds, which are earned by completing puzzles, and placing them in receptacles within the towers. Once you complete every tower, you can open a giant gate on the island and find out what’s hiding off on the horizon.

Everything that leads up to that final moment is important to the finale. Big Walk’s puzzles require you to effectively communicate with your teammates to complete them. Sometimes that means talking it out to figure out a solution, but House House routinely toys with your ability to hear or see one another. Some puzzles drop a soundproof wall between you and your friends, requiring you to get creative in how you signal to one another. Your characters’ very limited body language becomes crucial to that, as you need to learn how to convey an idea by waving your arms around or squatting. Savvy players can get around these problems with walkie-talkies and laser pointers, but the important thing is that you flex your problem-solving skills together.

Like any great video game, the ending is the ultimate test of those learnings. After walking through the final gate and approaching an orb, your squad is transported to a surreal tower filled with puzzles. The twist? You can no longer hear one another. Not only that, but all the colors that distinguish players from one another have been washed away. Unable to talk or even know who you are waving at, you’re left to solve a suite of puzzles based solely on the trust and communication you’ve built over time.

How to Use the Radio

Big Walk has a radio, not to be confused with the walkie-talkies. You can't use the radio to communicate with your companions, but it's still nice to have around. Not only does it play music, you can use it when you get lost. Turn up the volume and hope your friends find you!

How to Use the Radio
You'll surely stumble upon a radio during your journey; they live around the green transmitters. Push the red button on the transmitter and the antenna will go up, unlocking a radio station.

Until you find more transmitters, you'll only have that one radio station. There are seven radio stations in total, spread across the map.

How to Get a Backpack
By Kerry Palmer, Nicole Carpenter, Casey DeFreitas
Updated: Aug 11, 2026 11:10am GMT+8
You'll need to carry a bunch of things in Big Walk. A flashlight, map, and keys, to name a few. The problem is that your two hands can only hold one item. The solution? A backpack. Backpacks are hidden around the world for you to find.

Where to Find a Backpack
BigWalkBackpack.png

Each backpack and belt (which works like a backpack) will be in a circular blue structure. There's a pole in the middle, with one of these tools hanging on it. There's one of these blue structures pretty close to the Red Tower.

It's essentially a straight line from the front door of the Red Tower, walking toward the coastline. If you are on top of the Red Tower, look toward the entrance side. The blue structure is right ahead.

How to Put on the Backpack
You can't put on the backpack yourself. You'll have to ask a friend to do so. Have them pick up the backpack, then hover over your character with their reticle. Click to put it on.

Incidentally, this is also how you will need to place things in your backpack, as you can't reach it once it's on your back.

When you exit the game, anything you're wearing, like the backpack, will be waiting for you at home base.

You'll begin Big Walk without any instructions, and that means you're set off into the wilderness without a map. But Big Walk does have a map. You've just got to find it. You'll want to prioritize finding it first, especially if you're getting lost a lot. (You probably will!)

How to Get a Map

To get a map, you must leave the game's tutorial area and solve enough puzzles to unlock the Red Tower. You'll be able to see the Red Tower, because it sticks out among the greenery. Head to the Red Tower, and climb the stairs all the way up. You'll see a console table in front of a place to input all the red beans you'll collect.

Solve puzzles around the world (there are plenty nearby!) so you can slot five beans into the slots. A key will become available. Bring it down to the entrance of the Red Tower and insert into the key hole nearby. It'll shave down the key. Follow the arrow on top of the machine to find the next key slot. Eventually, you'll reach a big blue tunnel.

How to Save
By Kerry Palmer, Nicole Carpenter, Casey DeFreitas
Updated: Aug 11, 2026 11:11am GMT+8
Only one player in Big Walk needs to worry about saving the game. That's the host player. After hitting Host Game on the main screen, you'll be prompted to make a new save file or choose one to play with.

There's a prompt to name the save file. It can be anything, and always editable.

The next page will ask how many people are playing: two, three, or more than four. Choose the appropriate answer for the session. (You can switch between these whenever you want, with no harm to your save file.)

BigWalk-Guide-Hub2.jpg


How to Save
You don't need to worry about pushing save, neither does the host player. Big Walk saves automatically. Just exit, and you'll be able to continue your progress the next time you log on.

One thing to consider is that only the host player will have the save file stored, so you won't be able to access it unless they are online and running that session.
